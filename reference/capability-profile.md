# Capability Profile and Problem Spaces

## About this document

This is a **template you fill in for yourself**. It is not a finished profile. The frameworks in this repo (Founder Constraints, Market Opportunity, Runway & Sequencing, Income-Bridge Viability) all assume you know where your real right-to-win is. This document is where you work that out.

You record two things:

1. **Your capability profile.** The layers of skill, experience, and reputation you have actually built, and the rare combinations of them.
2. **Your problem spaces.** The specific places where those capabilities give you a defensible right to win, structured so each can be run through the scoring lenses on equal footing.

Fill in every prompt below in your own words. Keep it honest. The moat is real only where the capability is real. There is a short fictional worked example at the end so you can see the shape; delete it once you have written your own.

---

## Part 1: Your capability profile

### Capability Layers (building blocks)

List the distinct layers of capability you have built. A layer is a domain where you have done real work and can point to evidence, not a topic you find interesting. For each one, name the layer, give a short factual description, and state the single transferable capability it gives you.

Use this shape, repeated for as many layers as you genuinely have (most people have three to six):

```
**Layer N: <name of the domain>**
<Two or three factual sentences: what you did, at what scale, with what result. Use receipts, not adjectives.>

Core transferable capability: <the one thing this layer lets you do that most people cannot>.
```

Prompts to draw it out:

- What have you been paid to do, or done at real scale, for more than a couple of years?
- Where do people already come to you for help without you advertising it?
- What can you point to as evidence: shipped products, measurable outcomes, roles held, things built?
- What is the underlying skill beneath the surface activity? (For example, "operating under genuine consequence" rather than the specific job that taught it.)

### Rare Intersections (where the moat lives)

Individual layers are rarely rare on their own. The moat comes from **combinations** that almost nobody else has. List the intersections of your layers that are genuinely uncommon.

```
1. <Layer A> + <Layer B> + <Layer C>
2. <Layer A> + <Layer D>
...
```

Prompt: for each combination, ask "how many people in the world credibly have all of these at once?" The smaller that number, the stronger the moat. The intersections, not the layers, are what you build a business on top of.

## Part 2: Your problem spaces

A problem space is a specific problem, held by specific people, that one or more of your rare intersections gives you a real right to solve. Identify several, then rank them by moat strength.

Give every problem space the **same fixed schema** so you can score them against each other:

```
## Problem Space N: <short name>

### The Problem
<What is broken, and why is it painful? Be concrete.>

### Who Has This Problem
<The segment, and specifically who the buyer is, their role and context.>

### Why You Have a Right to Win
<Which capability layers and intersections apply, and what makes your claim credible. Receipts, not assertions.>

### What the Offer Could Look Like
Option A: <a services or done-for-you shape>
Option B: <a product or software shape>
Option C: <a hybrid, licensing, or training shape>

### Evidence of Demand
<What tells you people will actually pay? Signals, conversations, money already changing hands in adjacent solutions.>

### Risks and Weaknesses
<Where the moat is thin, where the market is early, where this could be commoditised or pulled into low-leverage work.>

### Capability Layers Used
<Which numbered layers from Part 1 overlap here, and how many. More overlap usually means a higher barrier to replication.>
```

## Key questions for framework analysis

Use these to stress-test every problem space before you commit to scoring it. They are the bridge between this document and the four lenses:

1. Where is the pain most acute and the willingness to pay highest right now?
2. Which space lets you charge the most, to people who already trust you, for work you could start this week?
3. Which space has the clearest path from services revenue to equity value (productisation)?
4. Which space builds the most durable moat over time?
5. Which space creates the most optionality for adjacent moves?
6. Which space are you most energised to spend the next five to ten years in?
7. Where does the "running toward versus running away" question land for each space?

## Worked example (fictional; delete once you have written your own)

The following is a deliberately simple illustration, not a real person. It shows the shape, nothing more.

### Founder Profile: example builder

**Capability Layers**

**Layer 1: Operations management (8 years)**
Ran fulfilment and logistics for a mid-size online retailer, scaling order volume roughly tenfold while cutting fulfilment errors. Built the standard operating procedures the team still runs on.

Core transferable capability: turning messy, manual operational work into reliable, documented systems.

**Layer 2: No-code and automation building (3 years, hands-on)**
Shipped a dozen internal automations connecting the order system, the warehouse, and accounting. Self-taught, but everything is in production and measured.

Core transferable capability: wiring practical software workflows together without a full engineering team.

**Rare Intersections**

1. Deep operations domain knowledge + hands-on automation building. Most operators cannot build; most builders do not understand the operational reality.

### Problem Space 1: order-handling chaos for small distributors

**The Problem**
Small wholesale distributors run purchasing and order reconciliation through email and spreadsheets. It is slow, error-prone, and steals a day or more a week from the owner.

**Who Has This Problem**
The operations lead or owner-operator at a 5 to 50 person distributor who personally chases purchase orders.

**Why You Have a Right to Win**
Layer 1 means you understand the operational reality these owners live in; Layer 2 means you can actually build the tool. The intersection is the right to win: you can design something that fits how the work really happens, not how a generic tool assumes it does.

**What the Offer Could Look Like**
Option A: a done-for-you setup of an off-the-shelf order tool, configured per distributor.
Option B: a purpose-built order-queue product sold per seat.
Option C: start with done-for-you, productise the repeated parts into software.

**Evidence of Demand**
Owners describe the problem unprompted; several already pay for partial workarounds.

**Risks and Weaknesses**
The niche is bounded. Document-parsing accuracy is the make-or-break. Off-the-shelf tools could close the gap.

**Capability Layers Used**
Layer 1 and Layer 2. Two-layer overlap.

### Moat Ranking (example)

| Rank | Problem Space | Layers | Barrier to Replication |
|------|---------------|--------|------------------------|
| 1 | Order-handling chaos for small distributors | 2 (Ops + Automation) | Moderate. The operator-plus-builder combination is uncommon, but the niche is bounded. |
