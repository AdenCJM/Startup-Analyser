# Founder Constraints

> **These are example default constraints. Edit them to your own non-negotiables.** The six principles below are a strong, opinionated default for a solo founder who wants to build a wealth-generating asset rather than a job. They are not gospel. Add constraints, remove ones that do not fit you, reword the tests, and reorder the tie-break ranking to match what you will actually refuse to compromise on. The system works as long as your constraints are honest, specific, and applied consistently across every idea.

**Use these constraints as scoring filters when evaluating any startup idea, business model, or strategic recommendation. Score each one PASS, Mixed, or FAIL. Ideas that violate your constraints should be penalised or eliminated, regardless of how well they score on other dimensions.**

The constraints are **conjunctive, not disjunctive**: a good idea passes all of them. An idea that is strong on five but fails one is still a bad fit for you, and the failing constraint is the thing to fix or the reason to walk away.

---

## How scoring works

- Score each constraint **PASS / Mixed / FAIL** with a one-line justification.
- Count the PASS results. The footer reads `Result: X/6 PASS`.
- **Founder Fit axis:** High = 5 or more PASS. Low = fewer than 5 PASS. This axis feeds the 2x2 quadrant in `market-opportunity-framework.md`.
- When two ideas both clear your constraints and you have to choose, use the tie-break order at the foot of this file.

A few parameters are yours to set before you score anything. Fill them in once, then the constraints below interpret against your numbers:

- **Your income-freedom target.** The recurring income that means you no longer have to take work you do not want. Set a figure.
- **Your bootstrap ceiling.** The milestone you want to hit without outside capital, stated as `$X recurring revenue within Y months on under $Z of your own money`.
- **Your exit horizon.** The window in which the asset should become sellable or runnable without you. The default below uses five to seven years.

---

## 1. Energy, not drainage

The business you build has to give you energy on an ordinary day, not just on a launch day. Short feedback loops, shipping things that work, watching real people use what you built, talking directly to customers. For most builders these create energy. Committee approvals, change management, proposals that sit in procurement queues, and managing organisational friction drain it. If the core of the work is the draining kind, the energy runs out long before the asset is worth anything.

Behaviour is a better signal than stated preference. Look at what you already do for free, on weekends, without being told to. That pattern is the truth about where your energy comes from. An idea that pulls you away from that pattern and toward work that depletes you will burn you out before it pays off.

**When evaluating an idea, ask:** what does an average Tuesday look like in year two? Not the best Tuesday, the average one. If the average Tuesday is meetings, proposals, and managing friction, the idea fails this filter. If it is building product, shipping, watching metrics move, and talking to customers, it passes.

---

## 2. Build for the model curve, not today's capability

AI models are improving fast across reasoning, tool use, agentic behaviour, and real-world understanding. Anything you build should be designed for where models will be in six to twelve months, not where they are today.

This has two implications. First, do not build a business whose value comes from doing something better models will soon do automatically. If the core offer is "I am the human who can wire these tools together," that offer degrades as models get better at configuring themselves. Put the value in a layer that models make better, not a layer they replace. Distribution, customer relationships, workflow knowledge, and outcome delivery hold up. Raw technical implementation does not.

Second, every model improvement should make your product better for free. If a new model release means your system handles more edge cases and needs less manual tuning, that is a tailwind. If a new release makes your method less valuable because the tools are now easier for anyone to use, that is a headwind. Ride tailwinds, avoid headwinds.

**When evaluating an idea, ask:** does this get more valuable or less valuable each time a frontier model ships? If the answer is less valuable, the idea fails this filter.

---

## 3. Win the game, then exit the game

You are building toward freedom, not a permanent job. Within your exit horizon (default five to seven years), the business should produce one of two outcomes:

- **Sellable.** The business is an asset a buyer will pay for at a price that funds your income-freedom target. Recurring revenue from many customers commands the highest multiples. A services business whose revenue is tied to you is worth far less. An advisory practice with no transferable asset is worth close to nothing.
- **Runnable without you.** The business throws off enough profit with a team running day-to-day while you provide oversight. This requires systems, product, and people who can operate without your personal relationships or hands-on delivery.

**When evaluating an idea, ask:** can this business exist without you in five years, and if someone bought it, who would they be and why? If there is no clear acquirer and it cannot run independently, it is a high-income job, not a wealth-building asset, and it fails this filter.

---

## 4. Bootstrap by default, avoid permissioned leverage

Default to building without outside capital. Not because raising is impossible, but because owning what you build lets you move fast and decide for yourself. Bootstrapping also forces discipline: you build what people will pay for, not what sounds impressive in a deck.

The deeper principle is to avoid permissioned leverage. Capital and labour are permissioned, someone has to hand you the money or agree to follow you. Code and media are permissionless, you build the product and ship the content and acquire the customers without anyone approving it. Prefer permissionless leverage in every decision. A higher-paying path that needs board approval or investor alignment is often worse than a lower-paying path where every decision is yours.

This is a soft preference, not an absolute bar. If the right idea genuinely needs capital to reach the scale where it becomes valuable, and cannot get there any other way, raising can be the correct call. But it is a decision made from a position of strength once product-market fit is proven, not a day-one decision from uncertainty. Any recommendation to raise should clear a high bar of justification.

**When evaluating an idea, ask:** can you reach your bootstrap ceiling (`$X recurring within Y months on under $Z of your own money`) without outside capital? If yes, it is bootstrap-compatible. If no, name the capital requirement and explain why it is unavoidable. Also ask: does reaching the first meaningful milestone require permissioned leverage (capital, labour, procurement approval, board consensus)? If yes, penalise it.

---

## 5. Revenue must decouple from time

You will not reach freedom by renting out your time, no matter how high the rate. Someone billing by the hour stops earning when they stop working. A one-person services business is a job with a different name on the invoice. Salary and savings alone grow far too slowly to ever throw off your income-freedom target on their own.

The business has to be designed so revenue scales independently of your hours. If doubling revenue means doubling your time, the business fails this filter. The path is code leverage: software that serves thousands of customers at near-zero marginal cost, not one person delivering thousands of hours.

This filter catches the drift the others miss. An idea can pass Energy (you enjoy the work), Model Curve (AI makes it better), Win and Exit (it is theoretically sellable), and Bootstrap (it is cheap to start), and still be a trap if the revenue model ties income to your hours. Consulting, retainers, done-for-you services, and fractional roles all fail this filter even when they feel productive and pay well.

**When evaluating an idea, ask:** if you took a month off, does revenue continue? If the answer is no, it is a high-income job, not an asset. Also ask: does the revenue model have near-zero marginal cost per additional customer? If yes, it passes. If each new customer needs a proportional slice of your time, it fails.

---

## 6. Wealth games, not status games

There is a recurring pull toward sitting in a more senior seat, running the thing, being the one in charge. That pull contains a signal and a trap.

The signal is real: you may have outgrown a role and the gap between your capability and your current leverage is genuine frustration worth listening to. The trap is that "I could run this" is a status claim, not a wealth claim. Wanting someone else's seat is a zero-sum status game. Building something only you can build is a positive-sum wealth game. They feel similar from the inside and lead to very different places.

The test is to run any "I should be doing X" feeling through the constraints above. A status move usually fails Energy (the average day is politics, not building), fails Win and Exit (you are building someone else's asset), and fails Bootstrap (you need permission to get the seat at all). When that happens, the feeling is just the last of an old identity dying. Let it.

**When evaluating an idea or an opportunity, ask:** is this a wealth game (building assets that earn while you sleep) or a status game (competing for a position in someone else's hierarchy)? If it is a status game, reject it regardless of how good it feels. Status is zero-sum, wealth is positive-sum. Build, do not compete for a seat.

---

## Using the six together

Score every idea against all six. An idea that scores well on five but fails one is still a bad fit, and the failing constraint is the conversation to have, not a rounding error to ignore.

If you have to rank them when two ideas tie, the **default tie-break order** is:

1. **Energy.** A drained founder builds nothing.
2. **Model Curve.** A business disrupted by better models wastes your best years.
3. **Win and Exit.** A business that cannot produce freedom is just a job.
4. **Revenue Decoupled from Time.** A business that ties income to hours is a job regardless of how it is structured.
5. **Bootstrap.** A preference, not a hard wall.
6. **Wealth vs Status.** A guardrail against the recurring temptation to chase recognition over freedom.

This ranking is itself a default. If protecting capital ownership matters more to you than energy, reorder it. The discipline is to decide the order once, write it down, and apply it the same way every time.
